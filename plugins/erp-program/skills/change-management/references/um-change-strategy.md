## 1. Program shape and dates

Two solutions, two clocks, one change team.

- **ERA** — pre-award grants administration. Go-live Fall 2028. Strategy text also references a January 2028 planning date for the illustrative timeline; treat both as unconfirmed and ask before using either in a deliverable.
- **ERP** — Finance, Supply Chain, and HCM. Coordinated go-live July 2029.

Staffing follows the clock: the bulk of the change team works ERA first while a small group builds detailed ERP component strategies, then resources pivot or split as ERA approaches go-live, and consolidate on ERP a set period after ERA is live. That transition timing is TBD.

Implication for every deliverable: label which program it serves. A stakeholder who is heavily affected by ERA (principal investigators, research administrators, sponsored programs) may be barely affected by ERP, and the reverse is true for payroll and supply chain populations.

## 3. Change model and phases

Six integrated components: Strategy and Management, Stakeholder Engagement, Organizational Readiness, Communications, Training and Knowledge Transfer, and End User Support and Continuous Improvement.

Phases map to ADKAR:

- **Define** and **Align** — awareness and shared understanding. Clarify impacts, engage early, connect the change to institutional priorities.
- **Enable** — knowledge and ability. Role-based training, readiness activities, hands-on experience.
- **Motivate** — reinforcement. Measure readiness and usage, address emerging barriers, sustain momentum.

When asked where an activity belongs, place it by what it produces in a person, not by calendar position.

## 4. Guiding principles

- Leverage input already gathered from UM leadership discussions in the Readiness Phase.
- Engage stakeholders early and often to build trust, surface concerns, create shared ownership.
- Communicate clearly, consistently, transparently: what is changing, why it matters, when to act.
- Design readiness and training around role-specific impacts and practical day-to-day needs.
- Use feedback loops to adjust as needs, risks, and project details evolve.
- Prepare the university to keep reinforcing adoption after implementation.

The strategy is explicitly a living document. Treat any parameter here as current best knowledge, not settled fact, and flag when a deliverable depends on something still open.

## 5. Change Network tiers and selection criteria

Three tiers: Project Sponsors including SteerCo, Change Champions, and Super Users, operating as a continuous feedback loop with the change team. Members are drawn from existing SME, advisory, and steering committees, supplemented with additional campus representation.

**Change Champions** are the primary tier and the main point of contact between the program and their area. They disseminate communications, facilitate change activities, provide feedback, answer local questions, and support security and training assignment in their area. The change team meets with them monthly. They also test ERP concepts with staff across campuses and hospitals and run early sentiment testing before broad rollout.

Selection criteria: well respected by management and peers with a strong network; people listen when they speak; strong knowledge of their area, its policies, business practices, formal and informal networks, and culture; open to change; can commit the required time.

**Super Users** are functional experts on current state who are open to change and support end user training. Selection criteria: deep knowledge of existing processes and systems in their function; already the go-to person for process or system questions in their workgroup or site; respected contact for internal questions and issue resolution; may serve as trainer, tester, or both; can commit the required time.

**Readiness Workshops** are hands-on sessions where network members see and use the system through predefined scenarios, each focused on a functional business process and led by functional SMEs. Purpose is confidence and advocacy, not instruction — do not design them as training.

## 6. Communication campaigns and channels

Core principles: consistent and trustworthy under a recognizable program brand; genuinely two-way so questions surface before misunderstanding hardens; tailored and role-relevant, tying system and process change to daily work.

Defined campaigns:

- **What's Changing** — educating stakeholders on process, system, and policy change. Emails, short videos, roadshows.
- **Training** — overview and expectations, course assignments, enrollment, logistics, reminders.
- **Cutover and Go-Live** — brownout and blackout periods in legacy systems, data cleanup tasks, go-live timing, how to access the new system, how to get help.

More campaigns will be defined. Every planned communication is categorized to a campaign in the plan so it can be filtered.

**MUHC cascade** (use this rather than mass email for health system audiences): Health Executive Leadership Meeting → monthly Directors meeting → monthly All-Leader meeting (manager level and above) → daily and weekly team meetings run by managers.

**MUHC tiered escalation** for surfacing issues, via daily huddles: Tier 1 front-line individual contributors, Tier 2 mid-level managers, Tier 3 executive leadership. Useful both as a feedback channel and as a route for urgent operational messages.

Prefer existing UM and MUHC vehicles, websites, and standing meetings over program-specific channels. Inventory them before planning.

## 7. Inventory field lists

These are the schemas the program's trackers expect. Use exactly these fields, in this order.

**Change Impact Inventory** — Change Title, Change Description, Current State, Future State, Impacted Stakeholders, Change Level, Perception of the Change (optional), Change Management Intervention(s).

**Communication Plan** — Communication Title, Description, Topics, Audience, Delivery Vehicle, Frequency, Delivery Date, Content Owner, Sender, Review Checkpoints. Maintained weekly; tracks timing, development, ownership, review status, and links to drafts.

**Training Curriculum** — Functional Area, Process Area, Course Title, Description, Topics, Delivery Method, Duration, Course Owner, Course SME, Security Roles.

**Stakeholder Analysis** — role impact, process impact, technology impact, degree of change, benefits, concerns, preferred communication channels, WIIFM.

A Change Impact submission form lets anyone submit an impact from any meeting, not only design sessions. When a user describes an impact in passing, offer to draft it in this format so it can be submitted.

## 8. Training modalities and systems

| Modality | What it is |
|---|---|
| Job Aids | Written system-specific guides with screenshots, step by step for a task or process |
| Instructor-Led | Live in person or virtual, recorded; blends presentation, demonstration, and hands-on exercise |
| eLearning | Self-paced online, process overview plus screen-level steps |

Sequence: Training Needs Analysis → curriculum → SME validation sessions → template approval → Training Design document per course, approved before build → content build tracked on the curriculum with weekly progress reporting → SME review and sign-off by area.

Delivery is train-the-trainer, with UM and MUHC staff delivering ILT to peers and functional SMEs present as the expert in the room rather than the lead trainer. Formal train-the-trainer preparation is delivered to identified trainers.

Two environments: **Training System** for development, delivery, and in-class exercises; **Sandbox** for self-directed practice in defined windows before go-live. Participants see only what their assigned security roles allow. Data is realistic with personal data masked per UM privacy policy.

## 9. Post go-live

**Hypercare** extends the implementation team through early production use, covering first-time operational events, stabilizing integrations and reporting, and resolving issues through defined intake, triage, and escalation with visible trend and resolution reporting.

**Stabilization** shifts from reactive support to normalization: assess usage patterns, reinforce standardized processes, address root causes of recurring issues, refine communications, training materials, and job aids based on real experience.

**Continuous improvement** requires the long-term operating model to be defined and validated during stabilization, with named ownership for configuration management, security administration, reporting, release management, business process governance, issue resolution, and training delivery and content maintenance. An ongoing training approach, including long-term content maintenance, is developed as go-live approaches.

Any AI-enabled adoption guidance supplements hypercare and support channels; it does not replace them.
